Scatter: State Min Wage × Avg Monthly Earnings
Each dot = one state · QWI EarnS estimate · 2022
ℹ
What this scatter shows States with higher minimum wages don't necessarily have higher average earnings — average earnings reflect all workers, not just those near the floor. A weak or flat relationship here is expected: the minimum wage directly affects the bottom of the distribution, not the average. A strong positive slope would suggest minimum wage increases are "pulling up" average wages in that state — evidence of spillover effects. r = Pearson correlation · OLS trendline QWI average earnings, all workers (A00), Q1 of selected year. Kaitz Index — Min Wage as % of Avg Earnings
MW monthly equivalent ÷ QWI avg monthly earnings × 100
ℹ
Kaitz Index with real earnings data Unlike previous dashboards that estimated median wages from ACS household income, this version uses actual QWI average monthly earnings as the denominator — making the Kaitz calculation more grounded in real payroll data. Kaitz = (Min Wage × 4.33 × 40) ÷ QWI EarnS × 100 A Kaitz above 50% means the minimum wage is more than half the average earnings for that state/group. High Kaitz values signal the minimum wage is a binding constraint on a large fraction of the workforce. Color: red >55% (very binding), amber 40–55% (moderate), grey <40%.
Kaitz from QWI-based earnings. Higher = min wage reaches further into the distribution.

Hiring vs. Separations
QWI HirN (new hires) vs. Sep (separations) · labour market churn
ℹ
Labour market churn and minimum wage High turnover (separations close to or exceeding new hires) is characteristic of low-wage sectors — exactly those most affected by minimum wage changes. When minimum wages rise, two competing effects emerge: some employers reduce churn as jobs become more valuable to workers; others hire fewer people to offset costs. Tracking HirN vs. Sep over minimum wage increase years helps reveal which effect dominates in a given state. QWI HirN = new hires entering for first time. Sep = all separations.
